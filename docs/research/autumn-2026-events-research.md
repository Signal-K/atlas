# Autumn 2026 Atlas event pilot research

Research date: 27 August 2026

## Recommendation

Run three deliberately small pilots rather than announce a multi-city tour
calendar immediately:

1. **Iceland anchor — 6–10 October:** a one- or two-night, small-group
   “Iceland at Night” experience based in South Iceland, delivered with a
   local licensed operator/astronomer. Use the aurora, volcanic landscape and
   telescope observing as the promise; never promise an aurora sighting.
2. **Tallinn/Lahemaa test — 17 October:** a repeatable 8–12-person beginner
   night-sky experience, with a city meeting point and a nearby dark-sky
   option. Make the base format work without a large equipment inventory.
3. **Estonia capstone — 6 or 7 November:** a darker-sky/photo-focused night
   near Tallinn, or an invited Tartu/Tõravere session if a host confirms. This
   is a good moment to test repeat attendance and referrals.

Keep Helsinki, Riga and Narva as partner/discovery conversations for this
trip. They should not become four more public promises before the first two
formats are proven.

## Sky and calendar logic

The useful lunar dates in the travel window are:

- 26 September: full Moon — suitable for a Moon/urban orientation event, not
  faint deep-sky observing.
- 3 October: third quarter.
- 10 October: new Moon — best dark-sky window around 6–10 October.
- 18 October: first quarter — good for a Moon-and-planets format.
- 26 October: full Moon — useful for a Moon-focused or family format.
- 1 November: third quarter.
- 9 November: new Moon — good dark-sky conditions around 5–9 November.

The Draconids peak in early October and the Orionids peak around 20–21
October, but the 2026 Orionid maximum has a roughly 72% illuminated Moon.
Market the Orionids as a seasonal story only if the actual observing plan
handles moonlight; do not use the peak date as the main sales hook.

Saturn is at opposition on 4 October, which gives the Iceland trip a strong
non-aurora observing subject. The early-October sky also offers a good
beginner narrative: Moon, Saturn, bright stars/constellations, aurora science
and practical night photography.

## Additional special-event concepts

### Strong hooks

- **World Space Week, 4–10 October:** an international outreach frame for a
  “Rocket Revolution” or space-and-sky event. It helps with partner outreach
  and press without requiring a rare celestial event.
- **Saturn at opposition, 4 October:** the strongest telescope hook. Build the
  evening around Saturn, its rings and moons, then add beginner orientation.
- **Southern Taurids, 4–5 November:** a low-rate but fireball-rich shower in a
  relatively dark-moon window. Sell it as a patient fireball-watch/photo
  night, not a guaranteed meteor count.

### Supporting hooks

- **Draconids, 6–9 October:** favourable moonlight and a good fit for the
  Iceland window, but variable enough to be supporting content rather than the
  sole reason to travel.
- **Moon–Mars–Beehive–Jupiter, 5 October:** a distinctive pre-dawn photo
  session for an optional early-risers add-on.
- **Moon–Jupiter, 6 October:** potentially memorable through binoculars or a
  telescope, but exact visibility varies by location. Verify locally before
  publishing it as an event.
- **Pleiades/autumn deep-sky night, 5–9 November:** a reliable, accessible
  binocular/telescope theme if the Taurids do not produce a fireball.

No obvious bright comet or eclipse in this travel window should displace these
concepts. Treat any new comet alert as opportunistic content, not a booked-tour
dependency.

## Central Europe option

The best central-European concept I found is a **Berlin + dark-sky reserve**
weekend rather than a city observatory event:

- **Westhavelland, Germany:** Germany’s first International Dark Sky Reserve,
  approximately 70 km west of Berlin, with existing public outreach,
  astronomy workshops and astrotourism infrastructure. A founder-led tour
  could use Berlin as the audience base and Westhavelland as the observing
  setting. The hook could be World Space Week/Saturn in early October, or a
  dark-sky deep-sky/photo weekend in the 5–9 November window.
- **Eisenwurzen/Hohe Dirn, Upper Austria:** an official regional listing calls
  out a Draconid programme for 8–9 October 2026, with favourable moonlight
  conditions. This is a good partnership lead if a local host can provide
  access, accommodation and safe transport; it is less suitable as a solo
  founder pop-up from Tallinn because of the extra logistics.

I would rank Westhavelland first for a central-Europe pilot: it gives the tour
an objectively credible dark-sky destination while keeping a large city
audience and partner pool nearby. The product should still be your guided
field experience; an observatory or local astronomy group can be a guest
partner rather than the thing you are representing yourself as.

## Place assessment

### Tallinn and nearby north Estonia — first repeatable market

Tallinn is the best base for demand testing, press, hotels and transport. The
city itself is light-polluted, so the product should either be an intentional
urban Moon/planet walk or include a transfer to a darker site. Lahemaa National
Park is about an hour from Tallinn and is a credible nearby nature setting;
confirm an exact legal, safe, accessible observing site and permissions before
selling seats.

Use Tallinn for the first public launch because it minimises founder travel
and makes cancellation/rescheduling less costly.

### Tartu/Tõravere — strongest institutional partner

The Tartu Observatory Visitor Centre has a 1.5 m telescope, Stellarium and
business/school programming. During autumn, winter and spring it operates by
advance booking on weekdays with a minimum group of ten. That makes it a good
private/group or partner-led session, not an assumed Saturday public event.

The Tartu Old Observatory and local astronomy circle are useful outreach
partners; ask them about an October/November evening programme before putting
their name on marketing.

### Helsinki — easy partner experiment, weak first dark-sky proposition

The Tallinn–Helsinki ferry is approximately two hours and runs multiple daily
departures, making Helsinki easy to reach for a partner meeting or an indoor
astronomy evening. The University of Helsinki Observatory offers guided group
tours (up to 20 people) with advance booking, while Ursa’s Kaivopuisto
observatory runs public shows when conditions allow. Treat Helsinki as an
indoor/observatory collaboration first; a same-day ferry plus outdoor night
observing is operationally fragile.

### Riga — compelling observatory partner, later pilot

The University of Latvia Astronomical Tower’s observing season begins in early
October and it operates on clear weekdays. Its public-astronomy credibility is
excellent, but a Tallinn–Riga trip needs an overnight plan rather than a
casual hop. Explore a November partner session after the Tallinn format has
validated demand.

### Narva / east Estonia — special trip, not initial city launch

Narva is worth scouting as a future east-Estonia story, especially if paired
with a darker rural site or local cultural partner. It is less suitable than
Tallinn or Tartu for the first public pilot because the host, exact observing
site, transport and audience acquisition are not yet validated.

### Iceland — premium anchor, with local delivery

South Iceland is the strongest initial base: it combines dark, open landscapes
with relatively accessible paved routes and daytime attractions. Around
Reykjavik, Heiðmörk/Rauðhólar, Grótta and Krýsuvík/Kleifarvatn are candidate
sites, but exact access, parking, wind exposure and permissions need local
checking.

Hotel Rangá is a particularly relevant partnership lead: it has a working
observatory, two high-quality telescopes and local astronomer-led observing
from September to April, weather permitting. A local operator should own
transport, safety and weather decisions. October averages about 0–7°C in South
Iceland, with rapidly changing conditions; road and weather checks are part of
the product, not an afterthought.

An Iceland trip could be one night, but two nights materially improves the
experience because the aurora is cloud- and activity-limited. The itinerary
should include an indoor astronomy/photography fallback and a clear
reschedule/refund policy.

## Event structure to validate

Use one consistent event record in the separate events product:

- one-sentence promise and audience (beginner, photographer, family, or
  enthusiast);
- date options, meeting point, route, duration and group cap (start with
  8–12 participants);
- what is included (guide, telescope/binoculars, transport, warm kit,
  photography help, food/drink) and what is not;
- darkness/sky targets and a plain-language weather decision deadline;
- indoor/shortened alternative, reschedule and refund rules;
- accessibility, footwear/clothing, toilets, transport and emergency plan;
- consent for photography and for linking attendees’ official-tour memories
  into Atlas;
- partner, guide, permit, insurance and cost-owner fields.

Sell a refundable deposit only after a minimum group threshold is reached.
Keep the first run intentionally human and manual; the separate events product
can later automate CRM, partner operations, booking, poster generation and
social publishing. Atlas should receive only confirmed official-tour metadata
for discovery, the journal/portfolio, and stamps.

## Why the founder-led format is credible

Position this as a **guided night-sky experience**, not an observatory tour or
professional astronomical lecture. Your value is choosing a safe site, setting
up modest equipment well, explaining what people are seeing, helping them find
targets, answering questions honestly and creating a good group experience.
You do not need to claim research credentials.

Describe yourself accurately as an engineer and astronomy enthusiast or an
independent night-sky guide. For advanced questions, cite authoritative
sources or invite a specialist guest. Observatories can be optional partners;
the field-led telescope/binoculars format is the core product.

Before charging for public tours, confirm commercial guiding, public-liability
insurance, land access, transport, first aid, weather decisions and participant
waivers. In Iceland, have a local operator own driving and route-safety
decisions.

## Go/no-go sequence

1. This week: choose the two public formats and a price floor using cost-plus
   economics; identify one Iceland operator/astronomer and one Estonia host.
2. Within seven days: publish an interest page with date options, not a full
   calendar; collect audience, city, skill level and photography interest.
3. Four to six weeks before each event: publish the confirmed date, collect
   deposits, and activate partner distribution.
4. 48–72 hours before: make the weather/road decision, communicate the plan,
   and move people to the backup date or programme if required.
5. Afterward: send photos and a journal prompt, invite an Atlas official-tour
   portfolio entry, ask for a referral, and record the funnel and margin.
