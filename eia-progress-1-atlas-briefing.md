# Atlas EIA Progress 1 Briefing

## Context

Atlas is a European Innovation Academy project: a consumer astronomy app for people who are mildly interested in astronomy but have no equipment and do not know what they can see. The strongest product wedge is:

> Wow, I can see awesome things and take cool photos, even with just my cellphone.

Atlas should be positioned primarily as a consumer astronomy app, with habit and community elements. The beachhead user is an astronomy-curious beginner with only a smartphone. Gear recommendations should be opt-in and should not be the primary investor/business-model pitch.

## Short Answer for EIA Progress 1

### What problem are you solving?

Atlas solves the beginner astronomy activation problem. Many people are curious about the night sky, but that curiosity rarely turns into action because they do not know what is visible tonight, whether conditions are good, where to look, or how to capture something satisfying with only a phone.

Atlas helps users answer: "What can I see tonight, can I actually see it from here, where do I point, and how can I take a photo?"

### Why is this problem important?

Astronomy is one of the most accessible science experiences in the world, but for beginners it feels intimidating and unclear. Light pollution, weather, moonlight, timing, and lack of equipment all create friction.

At the same time, many impressive targets are visible without a telescope: the Moon, bright planets, meteor showers, ISS passes, eclipses, auroras, conjunctions, and some bright deep-sky objects. Modern smartphone cameras also make simple night-sky photography more achievable than before. The problem is not only access to equipment; it is confidence, timing, guidance, and a satisfying first result.

### Who experiences this problem?

The primary users are astronomy-curious beginners who own a smartphone but do not own astronomy equipment and do not know where to start.

This includes city and suburban users who assume there is nothing worth seeing, students and young adults interested in space, families looking for low-cost outdoor learning, campers or travelers under darker skies, and casual phone photographers who want to capture the Moon, meteors, auroras, or the Milky Way.

### How frequently does it occur?

The problem occurs every time a beginner thinks about looking up at the sky but does not know whether it is worth going outside. There are nightly opportunities, such as the Moon, planets, stars, and ISS passes, plus recurring event spikes such as meteor showers, eclipses, auroras, and conjunctions.

This creates two usage patterns for Atlas: a daily habit loop around "Is tonight worth it?" and event-driven urgency around "Something special is happening, how do I not miss it?"

### Why is it worth solving?

It is worth solving because Atlas can turn passive curiosity into an active, repeatable experience. The user already has the core hardware: their phone. Atlas adds the missing layer: personalized recommendations, viewing conditions, pointing guidance, phone-camera help, reminders, and a personal observing journal.

The opportunity is not just another sky map. Atlas can become the beginner layer for consumer astronomy: the app people open when they want to know what they can see tonight, how to find it, how to photograph it, and how to build a personal record of their sky experiences.

## One-Paragraph Version

Atlas solves the problem that astronomy is inspiring but hard to start. Beginners are curious about the night sky, but they do not know what is visible, when conditions are good, where to point, or how to take a satisfying photo without buying equipment. Atlas gives them a personalized tonight plan, phone-based sky guidance, camera help, reminders, and a journal/community loop so their first sky experience can become a repeatable habit.

## References

- Falchi et al., "The New World Atlas of Artificial Night Sky Brightness", Science Advances, 2016. More than 80% of the world population and more than 99% of US and European populations live under light-polluted skies. https://arxiv.org/abs/1609.01041
- NASA Skywatching. NASA notes that the Moon, bright planets, meteor showers, and other night-sky events can be observed by the public, often without telescopes. https://science.nasa.gov/skywatching/
- NASA Spot the Station. ISS passes are location-dependent and recurring; NASA provides alerts and flyover schedules worldwide. https://spotthestation.nasa.gov/
- Liba et al., "Handheld Mobile Photography in Very Low Light", 2019. Computational photography can help novice phone users capture shareable images in very low-light conditions. https://arxiv.org/abs/1910.11336
- Public astronomy engagement survey, 2023. Survey evidence suggests many people occasionally watch the Moon, stars, or shooting stars, while very few own telescopes or astronomy apps. https://arxiv.org/abs/2312.13402
- Astrotourism / astronomy engagement research, 2026. Frames dark-sky and astronomy experiences as a growing interdisciplinary area connected to science engagement, culture, sustainability, and inclusive access. https://arxiv.org/abs/2606.00303
